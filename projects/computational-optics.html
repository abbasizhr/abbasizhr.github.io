Featured Project

Computational Optical System Modeling and Image Reconstruction

A diffraction-limited imaging system, simulated end to end and checked against theory at every step: from the pupil function through the point spread function and the modulation transfer function, into a detector that integrates and samples, through Gaussian and Poisson noise, and out again through three deconvolution methods.

The optics are written directly on NumPy and SciPy. No external optics library appears anywhere in the forward model, which is what makes the validation section mean something rather than being two libraries agreeing with each other.

The question behind it

Optical resolution is usually discussed with three different numbers — the Abbe limit, the Rayleigh radius, and the PSF full width at half maximum — that differ by up to 20% and get used interchangeably. Aberration severity is quoted sometimes in waves RMS and sometimes peak-to-valley, which for defocus alone differ by a factor of 3.46. Deconvolution methods get compared against each other when the dominant variable is actually the regularization parameter inside each one. And detector sampling is described as “losing detail” when what it really does is put real energy at the wrong frequency.

Each of those is answerable with a number. This project builds a simulator careful enough to produce those numbers, shows that it reproduces textbook diffraction theory first, and then reports what it finds — including where a widely quoted approximation stops working.

How it is built, and why that way

Four decisions do most of the work. Each of them exists because the obvious alternative fails quietly rather than loudly.

The pupil lives in image-space frequency

Not on a separate pupil-plane length grid. The focal length then cancels out of every quantity and never appears in the code. More usefully, the incoherent cutoff 2·NA/λ emerges on its own, because the transfer function is the pupil's autocorrelation — so checking the cutoff actually tests something instead of restating a constant that was typed in.

One place that touches fftshift

Every array is stored with its origin in the middle, where a human and imshow both expect it. Two helper functions absorb the shift pair and carry the area weights that make them exact inverses in physical units. Losing track of the origin is the dominant failure mode in Fourier-optics code, so it is confined to one file and tested there.

Boundary handling is an explicit choice

An FFT performs a circular convolution: light leaving the right edge re-enters on the left. For a grating that genuinely is periodic on the grid that is exact and fast; for a photograph it is wrong. So the convolution takes an explicit boundary argument with no silently-safe default, and the deconvolvers make the same choice.

Seeds are derived from labels

The noise for a condition comes from a hash of that condition's name, not from a shared random stream. It means adding one row to a sweep does not silently change the noise in every row after it — so two runs of “the same” experiment stay comparable.

Validation comes first

A simulator that produces plausible-looking blurred images has demonstrated nothing. Every one of the 21 checks compares against a closed-form result — never against a stored output of this code, which would only lock in whatever it did the first time.

The two strongest rows are the cross-checks. The optical transfer function can be computed as the Fourier transform of the PSF, or directly as the autocorrelation of the pupil; those are mathematically identical but share almost no code, so their agreement to 16 decimal places is a real test of the transform conventions. Similarly the Strehl ratio is computed once through a PSF and once through a pupil integral that never calls an FFT at all.

Aberrations: where a familiar approximation stops working

The Maréchal approximation for the Strehl ratio overstates the true value by a factor of 3.7 at a quarter wave RMS of defocus — 0.085 against a true 0.023 — and by orders of magnitude beyond that. It is a small-aberration expansion and behaves like one.

Four aberrations are swept at six severities each: defocus, astigmatism, coma and spherical. Severity is in waves RMS of a single Noll-normalized Zernike mode, with the peak-to-valley equivalent carried as its own column — because the conversion differs per mode, and a factor of three and a half is exactly the sort of thing that makes two aberration studies appear to disagree when they do not.

A second result falls out of the same sweep: the Strehl ratio and the MTF disagree about which aberration is worst, because they measure different things. At a tenth of a wave RMS of defocus — a level a Strehl of 0.663 would call “nearly diffraction limited” — mid-frequency contrast has already fallen by 40%.

Noise and restoration: the parameter matters more than the method

The spread within a deconvolution method, across its own regularization parameter, is larger than the spread between methods. And PSNR and SSIM do not pick the same setting, so “best” is not a property of the method alone.

Five treatments are compared across additive Gaussian read noise and Poisson shot noise. One piece of algebra is stated rather than glossed over, because it would otherwise produce a meaningless comparison: the “regularized inverse” that appears in most textbooks is the Wiener filter with K set to the regularization constant. They are not two methods. The naive inverse reported here is therefore the genuine division it is supposed to be contrasted with — and it is unusable, dropping to 7.2 dB where doing nothing scores 25.4 dB.

Richardson–Lucy has no explicit regularizer at all, so the iteration count becomes one. With no noise the metrics are still improving at 60 iterations; at σ = 0.05 the optimum arrives at iteration 3. A fixed recommendation of “20 iterations” is therefore wrong in both directions, by a factor of twenty, depending on the noise.

Detector sampling: a corruption, not a loss

Undersampling does not remove detail. It moves real energy to the wrong frequency, where it is indistinguishable from signal that belongs there.

The detector does two separate things that are routinely conflated. Pixel area integration is a blur with a sinc transfer function — a real, irreversible loss of contrast. Sampling replicates the spectrum, and when the pitch is too coarse the replicas overlap and fold. Measured across ten grating frequencies, the frequency at which a folded grating reappears matches the prediction to 3.5×10−10 cycles per metre.

How much folds turns out to depend on the object as much as on the detector. At four times the Nyquist pitch a broadband target loses 36.7% of its modulation to folding while an ordinary photograph loses 1.1% — not because the detector behaves differently, but because a photograph has little energy near the optical cutoff to begin with. A sampling study run only on photographs substantially understates the risk.

What the study found

  1. The simulator reproduces diffraction theory to a few parts in 104 across 21 independent checks, including two structurally different routes to the optical transfer function that agree to machine precision.
  2. The Maréchal Strehl approximation overstates the true value by 3.7× at a quarter wave RMS, and by orders of magnitude beyond. Quoting it outside the diffraction-limited regime is not a conservative choice.
  3. Strehl and MTF disagree about which aberration is worst. At 0.1 waves RMS of defocus the Strehl of 0.663 still reads as acceptable while MTF50 has fallen 40%. A single scalar quality number cannot stand in for the transfer function.
  4. Regularization strength matters more than method choice, and PSNR and SSIM select different settings — so reporting only one metric would have produced a different recommendation.
  5. For Richardson–Lucy the iteration count is the regularizer: the optimum moves from 60 iterations at zero noise to 3 at σ = 0.05.
  6. Deconvolution's advantage is not robust to PSF error. A quarter-wave error in the assumed defocus costs 22.9 dB → 20.7 dB — which is the honest context for every number measured with the true PSF in hand.
  7. Undersampling corrupts rather than degrades, and by how much depends on the object's spectrum, not only on the detector.

Limitations, stated rather than buried. The theory is scalar, so polarization is not modelled and the NA = 1.0 rows sit outside the regime where scalar theory is quantitative. The imaging is shift-invariant, so field-dependent aberration is absent. Deconvolution is handed the true PSF except in the mismatch study that exists precisely to measure what that assumption is worth. The pupil is sampled on a Cartesian grid, so its rim is pixelated — the dominant error near the cutoff, and the reason the analytic comparison is reported only out to 95% of it. And one figure's apparent result, the collapse of eight PSFs onto a single curve, is exact by construction because the simulation grid is defined in units of λ/NA; it validates the code's unit handling, not the physics.

Reproducibility

Every experiment is driven by a configuration file, uses a seed derived from a label rather than a shared stream, and writes its results as CSV and JSON alongside a record of library versions and platform. The figures are generated programmatically, the report tables are generated from the CSVs, and every number quoted in the prose of both reports is a macro written from those same CSVs. Change a parameter and the text changes with it; delete a result and the report fails to build rather than printing a stale value.

This was checked rather than asserted. Deleting the results directory and re-running the pipeline reproduces every measured value in every CSV exactly, and 17 of the 18 figures bit-identically. Running that check is also what exposed a genuine defect: one figure annotated a residual at the level of double precision, whose leading digit changed depending on whether a wavelength had been written 633e-9 or 633 × 1e-9 — one unit in the last place. It now quotes a bound instead.
